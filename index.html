Tharisa (THA)
Production Report for the fourth quarter and full year ended 30 September 2026
← Previous: NEUTRAL on 30 Sep
Tharisa's FY2026 production numbers are in — full-year PGM production and chrome output numbers to 30 September. Tharisa is the mid-tier PGM-and-chrome miner on the western Bushveld with a low-cost, high-margin chrome co-product that insulates it from PGM price weakness. FY2026 was a year of operational consistency. Chrome production held steady, PGM output was within guidance, and the balance sheet stays cash-flow positive at lower PGM prices.

Tharisa is the unsung compounder in the PGM space — a Bushveld miner that produces both PGMs (platinum, palladium, rhodium) and chrome concentrate from the same orebody — the Merensky reef that also holds chromite. The chrome co-product gives Tharisa a margin buffer that pure PGM peers don't have — when PGM prices are weak (they have been: palladium is off its highs, rhodium has pulled back), chrome keeps the lights on. Q4 and full-year production to September show consistent output from the Tharisa mine in the western limb, with steady volumes across both PGM and chrome.

The share trades at R26.50, stable through the year. The PGM complex in SA is in a wait-and-see phase — high stockpiles, weaker Chinese industrial demand, but supply constraints from Eskom and deeper-cost-push that are taking mines to the margin. Tharisa sits in the first quartile of the cost curve thanks to the chrome revenue offset. At ~5x EV/EBITDA and paying a dividend, it's priced like a PGM company but behaves like a diversified commodity producer. The full-year production confirms steady-state operation. Rating: BULLISH. Tharisa is a boring wealth compounder in a PGM sector that's full of drama. Boring is good.

The Foschini Group (TFG)
Cybersecurity incident at TFG London
← Previous: NEUTRAL on 02 Sep
TFG London — the group's UK retail division (Phase Eight, Whistles, Hobbs) — has been hit by a cybersecurity incident. The group hasn't disclosed the nature (ransomware? data breach? system compromise?) but cybersecurity incidents at a retail division mean operations disruption, data protection liability, and reputational damage. TFG has spent heavily on international growth — London being a key profit contributor — and a cybersecurity incident at the international division is a material risk factor.

TFG London is not a sideline — the UK division (Phase Eight, Whistles, Hobbs, and the recently-acquired The Vampire's Wife) is a material contributor to TFG's earnings and the growth engine that management has been investing behind as SA retail stagnates. A cybersecurity incident at that division is a material operational risk. TFG hasn't yet quantified the impact, but the typical cost of a mid-size retail cyber incident runs from supply chain disruption (can't process orders) to data breach notification costs (GDPR in the UK: meaningful fines) to reputational damage with UK consumers.

TFG shares fell from R50.27 to R49.20 on the day - down 2.1% - reflecting the uncertainty but not panic. The market is waiting for the extent disclosure. TFG has been a solid retail performer through the consumer squeeze - the JD Group acquisition was well timed and TFG's credit model gives it a moat. But a cyber event at the crown jewel international division is the kind of thing that can turn a solid year into a distracted year. Rating: BEARISH until the group quantifies the impact and confirms no material data loss or systems disruption. Cyber events at retailers always cost more than initial guidance suggests.

African Rainbow Minerals (ARI)
ARM to Defend Assore Legal Proceedings and Initiate Counterclaims
← Previous: NEUTRAL on 04 Sep
ARM is heading to court against Assore — the mining investment company it partly owns. ARM says it will defend the legal proceedings initiated by Assore and has launched counterclaims. Mining litigation is expensive, distracting, and seldom good for minority shareholders. ARM's stock dropped 6.6% on the news, from R156.50 to R146.24 — the market's immediate verdict on the legal overhang.

Litigation between a JSE-listed mining company and its underlying investee is a mess nobody wins. ARM — Patrice Motsepe's diversified mining group with interests in iron ore (Assore, through the Beeshoek and Khumani operations), manganese, platinum, coal, and chrome — is going to court against Assore. The announcement says ARM will defend the legal proceedings brought by Assore and has launched counterclaims of its own. That's an escalation, not a defence.

The context: Assore is a 50:50 joint venture between ARM and Assore Ltd (the separate listed company) that holds the bulk of ARM's iron ore interests. When the two JV partners go to law, it means the fundamental relationship between the operating partners is broken. Legal proceedings with counterclaims reads like the partnership has deteriorated beyond mediation. Translate: this is a heavyweight legal scrap that will cost time, management attention, and legal fees. ARM is a R35bn+ market cap miner with strong operations, but legal overhangs at the JV level are value-destructive. At R146.24, the market sold first and asked questions later — a 6.6% drop on the day is the market's first verdict. Rating: BEARISH. Mining litigation within the corporate structure is a cancer on the value of the underlying assets. Stay out until the counterclaims give clarity on the direction and potential cost.
